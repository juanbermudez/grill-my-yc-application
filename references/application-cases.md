# What the successful applications actually show

Checked 2026-10-19. These are instructive accepted applicants, not an objectively ranked set of the best applications. Historical numbers below are **application claims**, not current metrics or independently audited results. The commonalities are qualitative observations, not a controlled study of acceptance.

## 1. Dropbox — Summer 2007

**Evidence:** YC hosts the [original written application](https://www.ycombinator.com/apply/dropbox/), linked as a successful example from Graham's application essay.

Its opening is unusually easy to picture: “Dropbox synchronizes files across your/your team's computers.” The rest explains an automatic workflow inside existing tools. It names substitutes and explains the burden they impose, rather than merely promising a better experience. It distinguishes a working beta from a future chargeable version, and supports founder capability with prior projects.

**Borrow:** a concrete opening, the user's actual workflow, and a mechanism explaining the difference. Notice how technical detail becomes persuasive after the basic product is understood.

**Do not copy blindly:** several answers are lengthy, some are speculative, and the document uses a historical question set. Successful does not mean every line is optimal. Its pricing ideas are plans, not evidence of collected revenue.

**Editorial implication:** preserve specific insight even when cutting. Replacing this explanation with “cloud collaboration platform” would be shorter and substantially worse.

## 2. GitLab — Winter 2015

**Evidence:** [Sid Sijbrandij's company-published application](https://about.gitlab.com/blog/gitlabs-application-for-y-combinator-winter-2015/), published 2016-09-30, with redactions and later annotations.

It immediately identifies open source code collaboration, then uses a familiar product analogy. Its progress answers supply distinct measures: over 100,000 organizations, an estimate above one million users, $1 million annual revenue run rate, and approximately 60% monthly revenue growth. Its competitive argument concerns customers operating behind a firewall, source access, and concrete product tradeoffs.

**Borrow:** separate adoption from monetization; let actual progress carry the answer. Explain which customers an incumbent underserves and why.

**Do not copy blindly:** estimated users are not verified actives; organizations are not paying organizations. Run rate is not historical annual revenue. Its large existing footprint is not a minimum admissions threshold. Assertions about competitor limitations still need their own support in a new application.

**Editorial implication:** the proof has to survive the rewrite with its original labels.

## 3. OpenPhone — Summer 2018

**Evidence:** [Mahyar Raissi's published application](https://www.quo.com/blog/openphone-y-combinator-application/). The current page is dated 2024-08-13 and uses later Quo branding inside its answers. Treat it as a founder-published republication, not a verbatim archive of the submitted form.

It identifies a business phone product for small businesses, explains the problem of using personal numbers for work, and connects the idea to the founders' prior product experience. It reports 1,500 users, separately identifies 32 who had started paying, and lists $320 for the last full calendar month. It also explains recent billing introduction and ongoing trials.

**Borrow:** distinguish each stage of the funnel and explain timing that affects revenue. Connect founder experience to a specific customer problem.

**Do not copy blindly:** a field asking about active users receives a broad user count; the published answer does not establish an activity definition. Its competitive assertions and some market extrapolations are stronger than the evidence shown. Acceptance is no reason to repeat those weaknesses.

**Editorial implication:** preserve the useful numeric distinctions and improve any ambiguous definitions.

## 4. Coinbase — Summer 2012, selected application facts

**Evidence:** [Dalton Caldwell's official retrospective](https://www.ycombinator.com/blog/coinbase-from-yc-to-ipo/), 2021-04-14. This supplies selected excerpts and commentary, not the full application.

The application was submitted on 2012-03-29 under the name Bitbank, before launch. Caldwell highlights Armstrong's earlier bitcoin Android app and fraud-prevention work at Airbnb. The described customer problem is concrete: intimidating technical software and cumbersome buying/selling steps.

**Borrow:** show what the founder has already done and the exact friction a customer encounters. An early-stage company can establish credible technical capability without inventing traction.

**Do not copy blindly:** application-era bitcoin adoption figures describe the surrounding ecosystem, not Coinbase users. Later growth during YC is not pre-application traction. A later successful company does not make every early business assumption correct.

## 5. DoorDash — Summer 2013, video and interview account

**Evidence:** [Paul Buchheit's official retrospective](https://www.ycombinator.com/blog/doordash-from-application-to-ipo/), 2020-12-09, publishes the video and discusses dated interview notes. We analyzed the article's account; this is not a transcription of the whole video or written application.

At the April 26, 2013 interview, the founders had a website, no app, and 217 completed deliveries. They were doing deliveries themselves and learning from customers, restaurants, and drivers. Buchheit explicitly recalls continued uncertainty about the business and his own skepticism.

**Borrow:** a small real result and firsthand operational learning can be stronger evidence than a broad vision statement. Say what exists today.

**Do not copy blindly:** 217 deliveries is an interview-stage fact, not necessarily the count on submission day. Later success does not mean the partners foresaw it. Do not embellish a prototype into a finished product.

## Transferable distinctions

Across these cases, the clearest passages let the reader answer four questions: **What is it? Who needs it? What has happened? Why might these founders make it work?** The forms and business stages vary substantially. They share specificity more reliably than a particular length, vocabulary, revenue level, or narrative arc.

The important counterpattern is that accepted applications are imperfect. Some contain long explanations, broad claims, humor, or weakly defined metrics. Learn from their strongest evidence; do not build a superstition around every stylistic choice.
